## What and why

An open pit is a terraced excavation. Ore and waste are drilled, blasted, dug by shovels or loaders, and carried by
haul trucks up ramps to a crusher, a stockpile or a waste dump. The walls are cut into benches whose geometry trades
ore recovery against the risk of a wall failure. Every one of PitStudio's twelve cases works on some part of this
picture, so this page names the parts and writes down the first-order relations between them. The full derivations
live in [theory](../theory/README.md).

## The default site: Bingham Canyon from USGS 3DEP

PitStudio's scenes are built from real terrain. The default site is the Bingham Canyon open pit in Utah, covered by the
USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP): 1 m DEMs from 2013, 2018 and 2023 plus 69 lidar (LAZ) tiles. 3DEP products are
public domain, "free of charge and without use restrictions" [1] [2]. Two other sources add what Bingham cannot:

- **McKinley Mine lidar** (OpenTopography, CC BY 4.0, https://doi.org/10.5069/G9BZ6486) for close-up roads and
  textures. It needs a free OpenTopography key; without it, scenes use Bingham only plus CC0 textures.
- **Hambach (NRW, dl-de/zero-2.0) and EGMS ground-motion data** for slope deformation over a large open pit.

Where the lidar is too coarse to resolve individual benches, benches are regenerated by design code and labelled as
regenerated, never as surveyed. Real operations appear only as data sources, never as branding; see the
[Bingham 3DEP dataset card](../data-contract/dataset-cards/bingham-3dep.md).

## The production cycle in equations

### Cycle time

A truck's cycle is the sum of its phases [3]:

$$
T_c = t_{\text{spot,L}} + t_{\text{load}} + t_{\text{haul}} + t_{\text{spot,D}} + t_{\text{dump}} + t_{\text{return}}
      + t_{\text{queue,L}} + t_{\text{queue,D}}
$$

All terms are times (min): spotting at the loader, loading, the loaded haul, spotting at the dump, dumping, the empty
return and the queues at both ends. The queues are not inputs; they emerge from how many trucks share how many loaders,
which is why the cycle needs queueing theory or discrete-event simulation (DES), not a spreadsheet.

### Passes per truck

The number of bucket passes follows from the mass per pass:

$$
n_p = \left\lceil \frac{M_\text{target}}{V_b\,k_f\,\rho_\text{loose}} \right\rceil, \qquad
\rho_\text{loose} = \frac{\rho_\text{bank}}{1 + s_w}
$$

with $M_\text{target}$ the target payload (t), $V_b$ the heaped bucket volume (m³), $k_f$ the bucket fill factor (–),
$\rho_\text{bank}$ and $\rho_\text{loose}$ the in-situ and broken densities (t/m³) and $s_w$ the swell (–). Fill factor
is where blasting reaches the haul cycle: fragmentation and looseness set $k_f$ (see
[loading and terramechanics](../theory/loading-and-terramechanics.md)).

### Match factor

The match factor compares the rate at which trucks arrive with the rate at which loaders can serve them. Burt and
Caccetta define it and extend it to mixed fleets [4]:

$$
MF = \frac{N_T\,t_L}{N_L\,T_c^{\ast}}
$$

where $N_T$ and $N_L$ are the numbers of trucks and loaders (–), $t_L$ the time to load one truck (min) and
$T_c^{\ast}$ the truck cycle time without waiting (min). $MF < 1$ means the loader waits for trucks; $MF > 1$ means
trucks queue at the loader; $MF = 1$ is the theoretical balance.

*Worked example (illustrative fleet).* One shovel loads a truck in $t_L = 3$ min, and the queue-free cycle is
$T_c^{\ast} = 24$ min. With 8 trucks, $MF = 8 \times 3 / (1 \times 24) = 1.00$. With 10 trucks, $MF = 1.25$ and the
extra trucks queue; with 6, $MF = 0.75$ and the shovel idles a quarter of the time. Real cycles are random, so even at
$MF = 1$ some queueing occurs; [M01](../methods/m01-match-factor-queueing.md) adds the finite-source queue and mean-value
analysis, and [M02](../methods/m02-haulage-des.md) the full DES.

### Resistance, rimpull and speed on a ramp

Truck motion along a haul profile is one-dimensional. The industry decomposition is total resistance = rolling
resistance + grade resistance, the quantity that haul-truck fuel models are built on [5]:

$$
TR = RR + GR, \qquad GR = 100 \sin\theta \approx 100 \tan\theta \;\;(\text{small grades})
$$

$TR$, $RR$ and $GR$ are percentages of the gross vehicle weight (%), and $\theta$ is the road inclination (rad). The
force needed at the wheels at steady speed, ignoring aerodynamic drag (small at haul speeds), is
$F = m_\text{GVW}\,g\,TR/100$, with $m_\text{GVW}$ the gross vehicle mass (kg) and $g = 9.81$ m/s². When the engine is
power-limited, $P = F\,v$ gives the climbing speed $v = \eta P_\text{eng} / F$ with $\eta P_\text{eng}$ the power
delivered at the wheels (W).

*Worked example (illustrative generic truck, not OEM data).* A 400 t gross truck climbs a 10 % ramp
($GR \approx 10\,\%$) on a road with $RR = 2\,\%$, so $TR = 12\,\%$ and
$F = 0.12 \times 400\,000 \times 9.81 = 471$ kN. With 1.5 MW at the wheels, $v = 1.5 \times 10^6 / 4.71 \times 10^5 =
3.19$ m/s, about 11.5 km/h. Each extra percent of rolling resistance adds 39 kN and, at the same power, cuts the speed
by about 8 %. That sensitivity is why [A2](../cases/a2-haul-road-electrification.md) treats road maintenance and
electrification together.

### Energy to lift ore out of the pit

Lifting one tonne through 100 m costs $m g h = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 100$ J $= 0.273$ kWh at the wheels, before
rolling losses and drivetrain efficiency. Deeper pits raise this term linearly, which links pit design
([E1](../cases/e1-pit-shell-pushbacks.md), haul km and vertical lift per period) to haulage energy and CO₂.

## Slopes, the ground and monitoring

The stability of a wall is expressed as a factor of safety, available shear strength divided by mobilised shear
stress along the critical slip surface, minimised over trial surfaces; probability of failure adds the spread of the
inputs. Acceptance criteria depend on the slope scale (bench, inter-ramp, overall) and the consequence of failure; in
PitStudio they are user inputs, not facts, because the commonly quoted values could not be read in their primary text
(**UNVERIFIED — pinned at specification**). The methods are on [slopes and monitoring](../theory/slopes-and-monitoring.md).

Monitoring matters as much as design. On 10 April 2013, a slide at a large US copper mine moved 165 million tons of
rock. Radar measured wall movement every six to eight minutes; movement reached two inches (≈ 51 mm) per day before the
failure; workers were evacuated the morning of the slide and none of about 500 workers was injured, although
refined-copper output was expected to fall by 50 % [6]. The inverse-velocity method that forecasts the time of failure
from such radar series has published guidelines for smoothing and alarm thresholds [7]; it is the classical baseline
of [C1](../cases/c1-slope-time-of-failure.md).

## Mobile equipment, people and dust

- **Powered haulage is the leading hazard.** In 2025 it caused 13 of 33 US mining fatalities (39 %) and 426 of 4,792
  non-fatal injuries (9 %); MSHA estimates that proper seat-belt use alone could save three to four miners' lives each
  year [8]. MSHA's final rule *Safety Program for Surface Mobile Equipment* was published on 20 December 2023 [9].
- **Globally, mobile equipment is the most common cause.** ICMM members reported 42 fatalities in 2024, 9 related to
  mobile equipment and transportation and 5 to fall of ground [10]; mobile equipment caused 26 % of all fatalities in
  2021–2024, and 67 % of the fatalities in that period were linked to ineffective critical-control execution [11].
- **Haul-road dust** is estimated with the US EPA AP-42 §13.2.2 *Unpaved Roads* method, whose current section dates from
  November 2006 [12]. Its empirical form scales emissions with road silt content and mean vehicle weight; the constants
  and the applicability range are **UNVERIFIED — pinned at specification**, and the largest haul trucks may sit at or
  beyond the published weight range. See [dust](../theory/dust.md) and [C3](../cases/c3-dust.md).

## Tailings

A tailings storage facility holds most of the mass that enters the concentrator (see the
[mass balance](mining-value-chain.md#mass-balance-what-the-concentrator-recovers)). The 2019 Brumadinho failure released
nearly 10 million m³ and cost more than 270 lives; upstream-raised dams are clearly overrepresented in the accident
statistics [13]. The Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management (GISTM) was launched on 5 August 2020 with 15
principles and 77 auditable requirements [14]. A reproducible case study of the 2007 Miraí failure in Brazil (3.8 Mm³
released from a 34 m dam, peak breach outflow 422 m³/s, flood arrival in the town after about 2.5–4 h, about 1,033
people exposed, no deaths) shows what a run-out model must reproduce [15]. [C2](../cases/c2-tailings-breach.md) is
illustrative only: it computes arrival time, depth and inundated area for warning-time discussion, not for design.

## Assumptions and limits

- **Schematic, not surveyed.** The diagram is not to scale. Scene benches that the lidar cannot resolve are generated by
  design code and labelled as such.
- **Generic equipment.** Truck and shovel parameters come from academic sources, never from proprietary OEM charts;
  results describe a generic fleet, not a specific model of truck.
- **Simulation-grade.** No live telemetry enters the scenes. Cycle times, queues and speeds are simulated and
  validated against analytical limits (for example, the DES must reproduce closed-form queueing results in the
  exponential case), not against a real fleet-management log, because no open log dataset was found.
- **Educational outputs.** Slope, tailings and blasting results carry validity ranges and must not be used for design
  or regulatory decisions.
